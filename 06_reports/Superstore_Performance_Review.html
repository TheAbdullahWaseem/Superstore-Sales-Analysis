How to read this report

  1. Every section follows the same shape. A metric strip states the position, one or more exhibits show the evidence, and an Evidence → Reading → Implication block separates what the data says from what it means and what to do about it.
  2. Exhibits are numbered and self-contained. Each carries its own note explaining what the chart is doing. Hover any mark for exact values; charts backed by long series carry a data table beneath them.

Superstore converts 10.20% of revenue into profit across the four-year window — a defensible headline. But that figure is a net result, not a description of the business. Slightly more than half of all order lines lose money, and the profitable half is large enough to cover them with $1.52M left over.

The same pattern repeats at every level of aggregation examined in this study — region, category, sub-category, product, customer and year. Revenue rank and profit rank persistently disagree. The West is the second-largest region by revenue and the worst by margin. Furniture sells nearly as much as Technology and returns 2.27% against Technology's 14.81%. The second-best-selling product in the catalogue loses money on every unit. In 2012 revenue rose 8.24% while profit fell 10.37%.

Evidence
4,264 of 8,399 order lines carry negative profit, totalling −$1,102,503. Blended margin still lands at 10.20%.
Reading
Losses are not concentrated in one region, segment or channel. They are distributed and structural — a pricing and product-mix problem, not a discounting or logistics one.
Implication
The lever with the largest expected return is margin discipline on demand the business already has, not incremental sales volume.

What the analysis found

  • Furniture is the structural margin weakness. $5.18M of revenue returns $117.4K of profit — a 2.27% margin — with the damage concentrated in two sub-categories, Tables (−$99.1K) and Bookcases (−$33.6K).
  • Strong sellers can be loss leaders by accident. The Polycom ViewStation is the #2 product by revenue ($255.3K) and loses $17.8K. Nine other high-revenue products share the pattern.
  • Scale and efficiency are different regions. South posts the best margin (13.41%) on the smallest revenue base; West posts the worst (8.21%) on the second-largest.
  • Repeat business carries the P&L. 467 frequent customers — 59% of the base — generate $1.24M, or 82% of total profit.
  • Discounting is not the villain it is usually assumed to be. Correlation with profit is −0.04; average discount on loss-making lines is 5.2% against 5.0% overall. The apparent "deep discount cliff" rests on two order lines worth $1,002 in total and cannot support a policy conclusion — see §9.
  • Fulfilment is uniform, but not uniformly profitable. All three ship modes deliver in ~2 days; Delivery Truck carries 42% of revenue at a 4.33% margin against Regular Air's 14.72%.
Management implication

Growth strategy and margin strategy have been substituted for one another. The four-year record shows the business can add revenue without adding profit — 2012 proves it directly. Priority actions are product-level: reprice or retire Tables and Bookcases, put the ten loss-making high-revenue SKUs on a watchlist, and report margin beside revenue in management reporting so the disconnect cannot recur unobserved.

Business context & objectives

Seven questions a general manager would actually ask

Superstore is a US office-products retailer selling furniture, technology and office supplies to corporate, small-business, home-office and consumer buyers across four regions and 48 states. This study turns four years of its transactional record into decision-ready evidence, organised around one governing question: where is the business creating value, where is it leaking value, and which of those leaks is worth an intervention?

Interpretation standard applied throughout

Descriptive evidence is kept separate from business interpretation. A high-sales product is not automatically a good product; a low-revenue region is not automatically a weak one; and an observed association is not a cause. Where the evidence is correlational, the report says "associated with" and "warrants investigation" rather than claiming a mechanism.

Grain and scope

One row is a single order line — one product within one order — not a whole order. 8,399 lines resolve to 5,496 unique orders placed by 795 customers against a catalogue of 1,263 products. Percentages described as "of orders" in the profitability analysis are therefore percentages of order lines; this distinction is preserved wherever it changes the reading.

Data quality & preparation

Preserve business meaning; fix only what is genuinely broken

The cleaning phase was governed by one rule: an inconvenient number is not a dirty number. No rows were dropped. The 4,264 loss-making lines are the central signal of the study, and removing them would have made the dataset look healthier while destroying the finding.

The two extreme cases have different causes

Both outliers were traced individually rather than winsorised away, and they turned out to be unrelated failure modes: #353 is an operations anomaly (92 days in transit, low priority, trivial value) while #13088 is a pricing failure (fulfilment cost roughly eleven times the sale price). That distinction is the first piece of evidence that Superstore's losses have more than one root cause — a theme confirmed independently in §7 and §9.

Quality note carried into SQL

Dates and ZIP codes are staged as TEXT before conversion, so DD/MM ambiguity and leading-zero loss cannot corrupt the load. The normalised tables reconcile exactly to the cleaned file: 795 customers, 1,263 products, 5,496 orders, 8,399 order items, zero orphaned foreign keys.

South converts sales into profit 63% more efficiently than West (13.41% against 8.21%) on 14% less revenue. Central's dollar leadership comes from volume, not efficiency — it sits mid-table on margin. Ranking regions by revenue, as most sales reporting does, would place West second and conceal the largest regional margin gap in the business.

State and city detail

Illinois is the most profitable state ($108,532), ahead of Texas, Ohio, California and New York in a tight $86K–$94K band. Montana is the only state with a net loss — −$9,127 on $76,072 of sales, a −12.00% margin. That is not a small-sample artefact: $76K is real volume, and every dollar sold there cost the business money on average. Madison ($119,943) and San Francisco ($103,079) are the only cities above $100K, after which the ranking drops to a $53K–$66K band.

Why this matters operationally

Because the loss is confined to a single state rather than spread across a region, the appropriate response is a targeted order- and product-mix review in Montana — not a regional restructuring. Conversely, West's margin gap is regional in scale and warrants investigation of its product mix and pricing, since §10 rules out shipping speed as the cause.

Evidence
Revenue rank and margin rank disagree for three of four regions. South is 4th on revenue and 1st on margin; West is 2nd and 4th.
Reading
Regional revenue reflects market size; regional margin reflects what is sold there and at what price. They are independent measures.
Implication
Closing West's gap to South's margin on its existing revenue base would be worth roughly $190K a year — larger than the entire Montana loss.

The intuitive explanation for a 50.77% loss rate is discount abuse. The data does not support it, and testing that hypothesis properly is one of the more important things this study does.

Correlation
Discount against profit: −0.037. Against margin: −0.058. A genuine driver would sit near −0.5.
Loss-making vs profitable
Average discount on loss-making lines 5.19% against 4.74% on profitable ones — a gap of 0.45pp.

Discounting is applied almost uniformly across the product range, and profitability outcomes vary enormously within that uniform treatment. Since the input barely varies and the output varies hugely, the input cannot be the explanation. Discount policy is exonerated as a broad driver of losses. The evidence points instead to product-level cost and pricing structure — exactly what §7 and the Order #13088 investigation found independently.

Correction to a tempting headline

The −49.76% margin in the High band is arithmetically correct and analytically meaningless. It is computed from two order lines totalling $1,002.30 in revenue — 0.007% of the business. A "deep discounting destroys margin" conclusion drawn from two transactions would not survive review, and this report does not draw it.

The defensible discount finding is the Medium band: 748 lines and $1.15M of real revenue, where weighted margin falls to 6.50% against 12.60% at zero discount. That is a 6.1-point degradation across a meaningful slice of the business, and it is the finding that should drive policy.

The maximum discount anywhere in the dataset is 25%, and 82.07% of all lines sit in the Low band. Superstore is not a heavily discounting business. Among the ten deepest-discount orders, six are unprofitable and four are not — including a calculator discounted 10% that returned $2,052 on $5,196. Discount level alone does not determine the outcome; the product's underlying economics do.

Pricing recommendation

Do not introduce a blanket discount ceiling — the data gives no support for one. Introduce a conditional control: require approval where a discount above 10% is applied to a product whose trailing margin is already below the category average. That targets the Medium-band degradation and the §7 SKU list simultaneously, without restricting the 82% of business that is priced sensibly today.

Operationally, Superstore is remarkably uniform. All three shipping modes average almost exactly two days — Express Air 2.00, Regular Air 2.04, Delivery Truck 2.04 — and all four regions fall between 1.95 and 2.09 days. Shipping time has near-zero correlation with every other numeric variable in the dataset.

Two consequences follow. First, shipping speed cannot explain the regional margin gap: South is fastest (1.95 days) and West slowest (2.09), a 0.14-day difference far too small to account for a 5.2-point margin spread. Second, and more awkwardly for the business, "Express Air" is not measurably faster than "Regular Air" despite a name and presumably a cost that imply otherwise.

An extension beyond the notebook analysis

The notebooks compared shipping modes on total profit, where Regular Air's 75% volume share makes it the obvious leader. Recomputing on margin — from the same cleaned dataset — surfaces a finding the volume view conceals: Delivery Truck carries $6.22M of revenue, 42% of the business, at a 4.33% margin. Delivery Truck is the mode used for bulky Furniture, so this is very likely the same Tables-and-Bookcases problem observed through a different lens rather than an independent one — but it gives operations a concrete handle on it, and the overlap is worth quantifying directly.

The delay tail

Using a threshold of 8.94 days (three standard deviations above the mean), 105 lines — 1.25% of the dataset — qualify as unusually delayed. Order #353 (92 days) and Order #1699 (84 days) are the extremes; after them the distribution drops sharply to 31, 28 and 27 days, with the bulk clustered near the threshold itself. Regular Air appears most often among the delays, but it also handles 75% of shipments, so this is a volume artefact rather than evidence of unreliability.

The SQL benchmark query adds one operational lead worth following: while Furniture averages ~2 days like everything else, a cluster of Furniture orders consistently ships in 7–9 days, with a worst case at 17 days. That is a specific fulfilment pattern, not a company-wide problem.

Evidence
Delivery times vary by 0.14 days across regions and 0.04 across modes; shipping_days correlates ≈0.00 with every financial variable.
Reading
Fulfilment speed is effectively constant and can be excluded as an explanation for the margin variance found in §6 and §7.
Implication
Ruling a cause out is a result. It concentrates the remaining investigation on price and product mix — and raises whether Express Air's premium buys anything measurable.

In two of three year transitions the two measures move in opposite directions. 2012 is the clearest case — and the most recent.

2012 is the management warning in this dataset. Revenue grew 8.24% and order volume rose from 1,317 to 1,392, yet profit fell 10.37% and margin dropped from 11.10% to 9.19%. More business was written, and less money was made. Everything else in this report is an explanation of how that happens.

Four years produce four different peak months. 2012 grew revenue 8.24% and lost 10.37% of profit, with margin falling 11.10% → 9.19%.

Implication

Representative query — high-value, high-risk customers

This is the query behind the customer risk table in §8. It combines three CTEs, a percentile threshold and a company-wide benchmark to isolate top-quintile spenders whose typical transaction loses money.

-- Top-20% spenders whose average line margin trails the company average
with customer_metrics as (
    select c.customer_id, c.customer_name,
           sum(oi.sales) as total_sales,
           avg(oi.profit_margin) as avg_margin
    from order_items oi
    join orders o    on o.order_id = oi.order_id
    join customers c on c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    group by c.customer_id, c.customer_name
),
sales_threshold as (
    select percentile_cont(0.8) within group (order by total_sales) as p80_sales
    from customer_metrics
),
company_avg as (
    select avg(profit_margin) as avg_margin from order_items
)
select cm.customer_name,
       round(cm.total_sales, 2) as total_sales,
       round(cm.avg_margin, 2)  as avg_margin
from customer_metrics cm, sales_threshold st, company_avg ca
where cm.total_sales >= st.p80_sales
  and cm.avg_margin  < ca.avg_margin
order by cm.total_sales desc;

Representative query — top 3 products within each category

A ranked CTE, used because window functions cannot be filtered in WHERE:

with ranked_products as (
    select p.product_category, p.product_name,
           sum(oi.profit) as total_profit,
           row_number() over (
               partition by p.product_category
               order by sum(oi.profit) desc
           ) as profit_rank
    from order_items oi
    join products p on oi.product_id = p.product_id
    group by p.product_category, p.product_name
)
select product_category, product_name, total_profit, profit_rank
from ranked_products
where profit_rank <= 3
order by product_category, profit_rank;

A note on Query 6 — the strongest independent result

Labels is the best-margin sub-category in all four regions (25.95%–27.76%) and Appliances the worst in three of four, reaching −59.64% in the East. Consistency across regions is what makes this useful: a problem that appears everywhere is a pricing or cost problem with the product, not a regional execution problem — and it points to two concrete candidates, one to expand and one to reprice.

Two measures of margin, deliberately distinguished

Query 7 reports the average of line-level margins and finds every discount band negative, including zero-discount at −7.4%. §9 reports revenue-weighted margin and finds every band except High positive. Both are correct; they answer different questions. The simple average treats a $6 line and a $60,000 line equally, so it describes the typical transaction; the weighted figure describes the business. This report uses weighted margin for financial statements and flags the unweighted figure wherever it is the one quoted.

Limitations

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • Association, not causation. This is historical transactional data. It establishes that Furniture margins are thin; it cannot establish why without cost accounting the dataset does not contain.
  • Missing business drivers. No marketing spend, customer acquisition cost, inventory position, supplier terms, competitor pricing or overhead allocation. The profit field is taken as given.
  • The High discount band is not analysable. Two order lines and $1,002 of revenue cannot support any conclusion, and §9 declines to draw one.
  • Two margin definitions coexist. The Python analysis uses revenue-weighted margin; parts of the SQL layer average line-level margins. They disagree by construction — see §12. Weighted margin should be preferred wherever a single figure is quoted.
  • Seasonality is unproven. Four years, with a different peak month in each, is not enough to establish a recurring calendar.
  • Outliers materially move averages. Sales skew is +5.39 and shipping days +14.25. Every average in this report should be read alongside the corresponding distribution or ranking; this is why the analysis reports both.
  • Grain sensitivity. "50.77% of orders are unprofitable" is 50.77% of order lines. A whole order containing one loss-making line is not necessarily unprofitable.
  • Period. The data ends December 2012. Findings describe that window and are not a statement about current trading.
Interpretation standard

Where evidence is associative the report says so. Where a finding rests on a small sample it says how small. Where two valid methods disagree it presents both and explains which question each answers. A portfolio analysis that only reports the findings that flatter it is not demonstrating analysis.
